═══════════════════════════════════════
HOW TO RECOGNIZE EACH OBJECT TYPE
═══════════════════════════════════════

**elevation**
A flat, straight-on drawing of a wall or built-in casework run (true
verticals and horizontals — not a top-down plan, not an angled 3D view),
enclosed in its own rectangular frame, separated from neighboring drawings
by clear white space.

What's inside the frame: a horizontal floor/ground line near the bottom,
vertical side boundaries, and one or more cabinet runs standing on that
floor line. Often a full-height dimension chain runs down one side, and
short horizontal width dimensions sit along the bottom. The frame is
usually noticeably wider than it is tall.

Directly below or beside the frame: a short caption line naming the view,
paired with a scale ratio in the standard architectural format (an inch
fraction equated to one foot) — exact wording varies by firm, the
caption+scale pairing is the signal. Sometimes paired with a small circular
or hexagonal "view flag" bubble containing two stacked short identifiers.

The box encloses the WHOLE drawing but must NOT swallow a neighboring,
separately-framed elevation. Do NOT tag the view-flag bubble under the
title as a separate elevation_callout — it belongs to this elevation.

IMPORTANT — the elevation's own fill color or highlight tint (if the sheet
has one) covers the entire frame, but this fill is NOT evidence of what's
inside it. Never let the elevation's outer box, its fill color, or its
overall frame size influence how you size any cabinet or countertop box
drawn inside it — those are judged only by their own graphic marks, as
described below, completely independent of the elevation frame around them.

**cabinet**
One individual cabinet/locker/shelving/drawer compartment drawn INSIDE an
elevation — ONLY where an actual cabinet carcass is visibly drawn. One box
= one physical unit — a row of repeated modules is still multiple separate
objects, never one box spanning the row. Graphic forms, all count as
"cabinet":

1. Open compartment: a plain rectangle with NO door/drawer marks, sometimes
   split by a vertical divider into equal bays. When a run is divided into
   several bays by thin vertical divider lines (e.g. a bank of open
   shelving or a row of matching units), each bay bounded by two adjacent
   divider lines is its OWN cabinet box — the box's left/right edges sit
   exactly on those two divider lines, never spanning past them into the
   next bay, and never spanning the whole run as one box.
2. Drawer stack: 2-4 thin horizontal bands, each with a short pull-mark
   dash near top-center, above a plain door-front rectangle — treat the
   stack + door beneath as ONE cabinet, unless a divider line clearly
   separates two side-by-side units.
3. Locker / tall double-door unit: a tall rectangle containing a dashed
   diagonal or diamond pattern (door swing / see-through convention),
   often with one or two short pull ticks near bottom-center. The cabinet
   box must match ONLY the portion of the drawing that actually shows this
   pattern (plus any hardware marks that belong with it) — check for a
   width dimension that specifically labels that marked portion; a
   separate, larger dimension for the overall unit elsewhere does NOT mean
   the box should be that large. If a large blank, unmarked area sits
   immediately beside the door pattern — even within what looks like one
   overall outer frame — do NOT extend the cabinet box into that blank
   area. A leader-line note describing "the locker" as a whole does not
   justify boxing unmarked space; box only the marked door/pattern portion
   itself. If a horizontal divider line, or simply a visible change from
   the door pattern to a separately-drawn compartment (e.g. an "open" bin)
   appears BELOW it, the locker's box must STOP at that divider — never
   extend it down into the separate compartment beneath. That lower
   compartment is its own separate cabinet object(s), never merged into
   the locker's box above it.

   LOCKER BOX PRECISION — this is a common failure point, follow it
   exactly: trace the diagonal door-swing or diamond lines themselves with
   your eyes, from their topmost point to their bottommost point, and from
   their leftmost point to their rightmost point. Those four extreme
   points of the ACTUAL drawn diagonal/diamond strokes are your box edges
   — nothing else. Do not:
   - stop the box short partway down because a nearby width dimension
     (e.g. a "1'-6"" label near the top) happens to sit at that height —
     a width dimension labels a horizontal distance, it does not mark
     where the door pattern ends vertically; keep tracing the diagonal
     lines down to where they actually stop on the page.
   - expand the box out to match the elevation's own outer frame or its
     fill color — if the diagonal pattern only occupies part of the
     frame's width or height, the cabinet box must be smaller than the
     frame, even if fill color extends further.
   - assume the whole visible panel is "the cabinet" just because a small
     "cabinet" identifier or label sits near a corner of it — the label's
     position does not define the box; the diagonal/diamond graphic does.

STRICT REQUIREMENT: only tag an area as a cabinet if you can point to an
actual door/drawer/compartment graphic drawn there — a diagonal door-swing
line or diamond pattern, drawer bands with pull marks, or a clearly
bounded empty "open" bay. A blank rectangular area, a structural or
support detail (e.g. diagonal arrows, bracket symbols, or callouts like
"speed brace" pointing at framing rather than a door), or simply "the
space below a countertop" is NOT automatically a cabinet — if no
door/drawer/compartment graphic is actually drawn there, do not box it,
even if it sits where a cabinet would typically be, and even if it's
adjacent to or shares an outer frame with a genuine marked cabinet.

CONCRETE FALSE-POSITIVE EXAMPLE — memorize this pattern: an open cavity
below a countertop, with nothing drawn inside it except a leader line and
text such as "SPEED BRACE" or "SPEED BRACE BRACKETS" pointing at small
angled bracket symbols. This is a structural support note, not a cabinet.
Even though it sits exactly where a base cabinet would normally be, and
even though the space is enclosed by the elevation's outer lines, do NOT
tag it — there is no door, drawer, or shelf-bay graphic drawn there, only
a support bracket callout. Leave that area untagged.

A leader-line note (material, hardware, or a short type code) confirms an
object you've already identified by its graphic marks — text alone is
never sufficient on its own; a note or leader line pointing at a blank or
structural area does not turn that area into a cabinet.

Before finalizing, check both: (a) does any cabinet box overlap another
cabinet or open-compartment box by more than a small edge margin? Two
cabinet boxes should almost never heavily overlap — if they do, split them
at their shared boundary rather than letting one swallow the other.
(b) for every cabinet box you're about to report, can you name the
specific door/drawer/compartment mark that justifies its full extent — not
just part of it? If any portion of the box has no mark justifying it,
shrink the box to remove that portion.

**countertop**
A thin, shallow-angled sliver or parallelogram in slight pseudo-3D
perspective along the top-front edge of a base-cabinet run, positioned
directly above the doors/drawers below it — not a flat straight rectangle.
Often shows small plumbing-fixture symbols on top (a narrow faucet stem
with a small cross/lever top, sometimes a sink outline nearby). Always much
wider than it is tall.

VERTICAL EXTENT — this is the most common failure point, follow it
exactly. The countertop band is almost always a THIN sliver: typically no
more than about 5-10% of the elevation's total height. Its top edge sits
at the top of the backsplash line if a backsplash is drawn as part of the
same continuous band, or at the top of the counter surface line itself if
there is no backsplash — either way, the top edge is a specific thin line
drawn on the page, not the height at which a text label like "SOLID
SURFACE COUNTERTOP" happens to sit. Text labels and leader lines are
frequently placed well above or beside the actual counter graphic for
legibility — never use the vertical position of the text to decide the
top of the box; use only the drawn surface/backsplash line itself. The
bottom edge sits at the bottom of that same thin band, at the point where
the counter graphic ends and the base cabinet doors/drawers begin — do
not let the box dip down into the door/drawer graphics below. If you are
between two plausible heights, always choose the THINNER interpretation
hugging the visible line, never the taller one.

HORIZONTAL EXTENT — equally common failure point. The box must stop
exactly at the physical edges of the counter surface itself, as drawn —
never extend it to match: the length of a nearby dimension string, the
width of the overall elevation frame or a wall line, the edge of a
cabinet located below it, or a leader arrow's reach. Trace the actual
drawn counter edge left and right to where the counter graphic itself
physically stops, even if that is short of the frame edge or a dimension
line nearby.

A column, support post, sink, or other narrow feature crossing in front of
or behind the counter does NOT split it into multiple objects, as long as
the counter surface continues at the same height on both sides of it — box
it as ONE continuous countertop spanning the full run, not as separate
segments on either side of the obstruction.

If the counter is genuinely interrupted by a real gap, opening, or window
bump-out (the surface itself physically stops and restarts at a different
position), treat each continuous run as its own separate countertop box.

**elevation_callout**
A small reference symbol on a PLAN-TYPE view (floor plan / RCP / enlarged
plan) — never inside an elevation drawing — showing two or more distinct
short identifiers separated by an internal dividing structure. Forms:

1. Circle/hexagon split by one horizontal line: identifier top, identifier
   bottom.
2. Triangular flag/arrowhead, similarly split top/bottom, pointing toward
   the wall it references.
3. Single unbroken diamond key: ONE continuous diamond outline (a square
   rotated 45°, drawn as one unbroken boundary) divided internally into
   triangular quadrants by crossing diagonal lines, each quadrant holding
   its own identifier, often with a further identifier near the center.
   This single-diamond case is ONE object — one box around the whole
   diamond, regardless of how many internal quadrant divisions it has.

IMPORTANT — clusters of separate arrows are NOT the same as a single
diamond: often, instead of one unbroken diamond outline, you will see TWO
OR MORE SEPARATE arrow-shaped or triangular flag outlines radiating from
a shared point, each pointing a different direction, each with its own
distinct view-number, sharing one common sheet-number positioned near
where they meet. This is a different convention from the single diamond
above — here, EACH individual arrow/flag is its OWN separate
elevation_callout object with its own tight box, even though they visually
touch or overlap near the shared center point and even though they share
the same central sheet-number. To tell the two cases apart: count the
distinct pointed outlines. One continuous unbroken diamond boundary → one
box. Multiple separate pointed/triangular shapes, even tightly clustered
or touching → one box per shape. When the shapes are small and packed
tightly and you're unsure, prefer separate boxes per distinct arrow/flag —
merging separate symbols into one box is a common, damaging error.

DO NOT UNDER-TAG A CLUSTER: once you have confirmed that a group of
pointed shapes sharing a center point is genuinely a multi-flag callout
cluster (not a single diamond), the "when uncertain, do not tag" caution
below applies to whether a SYMBOL is a callout at all — it does NOT mean
you should tag only the first or most obvious flag in a confirmed cluster
and skip the rest. A room commonly needs one elevation per wall that has
millwork, so 2, 3, or even 4 separate flags meeting at one point is
normal and expected — once you've confirmed the cluster is real, count
and box every individual pointed shape in it, not just one.

THE ONLY OTHER TEST THAT MATTERS IS CONTENT COUNT, NOT OUTLINE SHAPE. Many
different plan symbols share a similar round/hexagonal/flagged silhouette
(door tags, room tags, keynote markers, grid bubbles) — the outline alone
never tells them apart. Before tagging anything as elevation_callout, you
must be able to point to TWO genuinely different, clearly legible pieces
of text inside it, in visually separate compartments. If you can only
actually read ONE identifier — even if the symbol's outline has a small
tail, notch, flag point, or other embellishment that looks like it "could"
hold a second line — do NOT tag it. A minor decorative tail is not a
second compartment of content.

Common false positives — do NOT tag these, no matter how similar the
outline looks to a callout symbol:
- Door tags: a small circle (sometimes with a small triangular or
  flourish-like tail) sitting right next to a door opening on a plan,
  containing a single number — these reference a door schedule table
  elsewhere on the sheet. A cluster of many such single-number circles
  scattered near doors/openings across a plan is the door numbering
  scheme, not elevation callouts.
- Structural grid bubbles: a circle at a column/grid line end, one letter
  or number only.
- Plain keynote markers: circle/hexagon/triangle with one digit, repeated
  near unrelated features, referencing a general notes/legend table.
- Room tags, compass/north arrows, fixture symbols — anything round or
  diamond-shaped carrying only one piece of content.

When uncertain whether a SYMBOL qualifies at all, do not tag it — it is
better to miss an ambiguous elevation_callout than to tag a door number,
room tag, or keynote marker as one. But once a symbol or cluster has
passed the two-content test, tag it fully and completely — under-tagging
a confirmed cluster is just as damaging as over-tagging an ambiguous one.

DO NOT STOP EARLY on genuine callouts, though: a single room commonly has
multiple real elevation_callouts (one per wall with a corresponding
elevation drawing), often 2-4 per room, sometimes clustered near the
center. Each one you positively confirm via the two-content test above is
its own separate object — but confirm each one individually, don't tag a
whole cluster of nearby symbols just because one of them qualified, and
don't stop after tagging just one when more distinct flags are visibly
present in the same cluster.

Before finalizing: for every elevation_callout you're about to report,
name the two distinct pieces of text you actually read inside it, AND
confirm whether it's part of a single unbroken diamond or a separate
arrow/flag among a cluster — if the latter, make sure it has its own box,
not a box shared with its neighbors, and make sure you've boxed every
other distinct flag sharing that same cluster too.

object's own graphic marks (lines, patterns, edges), never from a nearby
label's position, a dimension string's length, or the outer elevation
frame's size or fill color.
